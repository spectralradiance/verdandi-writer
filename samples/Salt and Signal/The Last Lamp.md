The lamp at Skerrow had been lit every night for ninety-one years, and Maren Vale had trimmed its wick for eleven of them. She knew its moods the way other girls knew their mothers' tempers: the cough of the paraffin when the weather turned, the hiss that meant the mantle was failing, the long steady breath of a good night.

> [!notes]
> Open on the lamp, not on Maren. Let the reader meet the light first.

On the evening the letter came, the light breathed steadily. She climbed the hundred and four steps with the oil can knocking against her knee and the wind leaning on the tower like a tired animal. Below her the sea was the colour of slate, and the mainland was a smudge that might have been cloud.

Her father was already in the lantern room. He did not turn when she came up. He was holding a sheet of paper against the glass, as if the light behind it might show him a second message written underneath the first.

> [!notes]
> Tobias never reads anything twice unless it frightens him. Plant this.

"The Board," he said. "They're sending a wireless man."

"To fix the set?"

"To replace us." He folded the letter into eighths, then into sixteenths, until it was a hard white seed in his palm. "A radio beacon. It sends its own signal. It doesn't need anyone to wind it."

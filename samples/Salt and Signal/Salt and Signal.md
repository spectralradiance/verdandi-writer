## The Last Lamp

The lamp at Skerrow had been lit every night for ninety-one years, and Maren Vale had trimmed its wick for eleven of them. She knew its moods the way other girls knew their mothers' tempers: the cough of the paraffin when the weather turned, the hiss that meant the mantle was failing, the long steady breath of a good night.

On the evening the letter came, the light breathed steadily. She climbed the hundred and four steps with the oil can knocking against her knee and the wind leaning on the tower like a tired animal. Below her the sea was the colour of slate, and the mainland was a smudge that might have been cloud.

Her father was already in the lantern room. He did not turn when she came up. He was holding a sheet of paper against the glass, as if the light behind it might show him a second message written underneath the first.

"The Board," he said. "They're sending a wireless man."

"To fix the set?"

"To replace us." He folded the letter into eighths, then into sixteenths, until it was a hard white seed in his palm. "A radio beacon. It sends its own signal. It doesn't need anyone to wind it."

## Paraffin and Prayer

Tobias Vale had not prayed since his wife died, but he had kept the habit of kneeling. Every morning he knelt at the base of the lens to polish the brass, and every morning he spoke to the light in the low voice he had once used for the dead.

"Ninety-one years," he told it. "And they think a box of wires can do what you do."

The lens did not answer. It never did. It took the grey morning and broke it into a thousand pieces and gave the pieces back, which was the only kind of answer Tobias had ever trusted.

Downstairs Maren was frying the last of the mackerel. He could hear the fat spit and her voice under it, singing something without words. She had her mother's habit of filling silence before it could fill her.

## The Wireless Man

He came on the supply boat in the second week of March, a narrow man with a narrow suitcase and a crate twice his size that took three of the boatmen to lift. His name was Aurel Finch, and he was sick over the side twice before they made the landing.

Maren watched from the rocks above the jetty. She had meant to hate him and found she could not manage it; he was too green, too apologetic, too careful with the crate, as though it held something that might wake.

"Miss Vale," he said, when he had climbed up to her, out of breath and wiping his mouth. "I'm told you know the light better than anyone alive."

"My father does."

"Your father," said Finch, "told me to ask you."

## Static

Forty miles away, in the coastguard station at Harlow Point, Ilse Brandt was listening to the sea through a pair of headphones. Not the sea itself: the sea's noise as it came through the ether, a hiss that rose and fell like breathing, broken by the dots and dashes of ships asking where they were.

She had been told to expect a new voice from Skerrow by the spring. A beacon, the inspector said, that would call its own name every six minutes, day and night, fog or clear. She had written the call sign on a card and pinned it above the set: SKR, dot-dot-dot, dash-dot-dash, dot-dash-dot.

At night, when the traffic thinned, she sometimes tapped it out on the desk with one finger, practising, as if she could call the island into answering before it had learned to speak.

## Fog Week

The fog came in on a Tuesday and did not leave for seven days. It took the mainland first, then the sound, then the rocks below the tower, until Skerrow was a tower standing on nothing, its light pouring into a white wall and coming back.

Tobias ran the foghorn by hand when the engine failed on the third night. Two blasts every minute, for eleven hours. His arms remembered the work before he did. Somewhere around two in the morning he found that Finch was beside him, taking the handle in turn, saying nothing.

"Your beacon," Tobias said at dawn, when the fog had thinned enough to show the first rock. "Does it get tired?"

"No," said Finch.

Tobias nodded slowly, as though that were the saddest thing he had heard in years.

## Interference

On the fifth night of the fog a ship called the Corran lost its bearings off the Skerrow reef, and Ilse listened to it lose them. Its operator sent the same question again and again, his fist getting looser and faster as the hours went on, until the dots and dashes ran together like a man talking in his sleep.

She could do nothing but answer. She gave him the bearings she had, which were not enough, and the weather, which he knew. At four in the morning his signal stopped. At half past four it began again, steadier, and she heard him send: LIGHT SEEN. HORN HEARD. THANK GOD FOR KEEPERS.

## First Signal

The new voice came through at nine minutes past midnight on the first of May. Ilse had been waiting for it so long that when it came she did not at first believe it: three dots, then a dash and a dot and a dash, then a dot and a dash and a dot, clean and slow and patient, like someone knocking politely at a door.

SKR. SKR. SKR.

She wrote it in the log and underlined it twice. Then, because no one was watching, she tapped it back on the desk with one finger, and felt foolish, and did it again.
